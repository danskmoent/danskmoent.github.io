The silver and billon standard of Denmark and Norway under King Hans (1481–1513): the Lübeck system without a Danish Møntordning of its own. After the coinage union with the Wendish towns broke up, Danish coinage remained dependent on that of the towns[16]. The measure of this system is the Schilling: on 15 June 1468 Hamburg and Lübeck set it at 103½ pieces from the 9-lot mark, and the recesses of 1483 and 1492 reaffirmed this standard[1] — that is, 11½ Mark lübisch (the money of account of 16 Schilling) per fine mark[17]; the weight mark is the Cologne mark, identical with the Lübeck mark for silver coin[18]. No Danish Møntordning of King Hans has been preserved[4]. The Haandfæstning of 1483 bound the Norwegian mints to «jaffngod mynt wid Danske penninge» («coin equal in value to Danish pennies»)[2]; Wilcke reads this as coinage «efter lybsk Møntfod» («on the Lübeck standard»)[3].

Hans’ Skilling and Søsling corresponded well in weight to the Lübeck pieces; the Hvid stayed markedly lighter than the Lübeck Witten and passed for only 3 Lübeck pennies[12]. The Rhenish gulden, reckoned at 2 Mark dansk at the beginning of the reign, rose to 2½ Mark dansk around the turn of the century[5].

The Haandfæstning of Christian II of 22 July 1513 sets two Mark dansk to one Rhenish gulden[6]; the Møntordning of 1514 introduces a new silver standard with the Sølvgylden (8½-Gylden-Fod). The gold of the same king follows Nobelfod and Guldgyldenfod.

Danish-Norwegian gold standard: coinage 1496–1532, legal basis from 1514. King Hans strikes the first Danish Nobel in 1496[30] — the first dated gold coin of Denmark[31]. The Nobel takes its name from the noble metal[32]. Its model is the great Dutch gold real (groote gouden reaal), struck in Holland in 1487 for the Roman King, later Emperor Maximilian; the Nobel match it closely in design and weight and perhaps also in fineness[33]. The gold coinage under Hans — Noble and Gylden — is probably connected with the armament for the Swedish campaign[34]. According to Galster, the Nobel of Hans, like those of Christian II, are first and foremost «Pragtmønter» (show coins)[35]; Wilcke places the gold coinages of 1496, 1516 and 1532 in the context of wartime[36].

No Danish ordinance for the Nobel is known before 1514. The Møntordning Christian II of Summer 1514 specifies the Nobel[26] and the Rhinsk Gylden (Guldgyldenfod)[37]. The fineness of the Nobel is prescribed by the ordinances: no determination of the fineness of a Danish Nobel is attested in the sources, and for the Nobel of 1516 and 1518 Galster expressly regards it as uncertain whether they meet the prescribed fineness[38]. In 1524 the fineness is changed twice: the Council of the Realm (Rigsraad) lowers it by half a carat, from 23½ to 23[39][27], and Frederik I’s Møntordning of 1524 (Kongebrev) restores the previous fineness the same year[40][29].

The line is a series of rare individual issues under three rulers[41]. Christian III (1534–1559) does not continue the line, and no Nobel coinage after 1532 is documented[30].

Guldgyldenfod — the Danish line of the Rhenish gold gulden, the leading gold trade and account coin of the Holy Roman Empire[85]. The imperial norm — 71⅓ pieces from the rough Cologne mark at 18½ carats — was agreed in 1490 by the four Rhenish electors and made binding throughout the Empire by the Reichsabschied of Worms in 1495[79][86]; Karl V's Esslingen Reichsmünzordnung of 1524 replaced it with a lesser gulden[87], probably for want of gold[88], in 1551 Karl V restored the old form[89], and Ferdinand I's coinage edict of 19 August 1559 finally fixed 72 pieces from the 18½-carat mark[81].

The oldest surviving Danish instrument on the gulden is Christian II's letter of appointment for the mintmaster Dynes Blicher of 1 August 1513: no Danish Møntordning of King Hans is preserved[4], and according to Galster the ordinance of 1513 merely repeats, in the points concerned, provisions of older, lost Danish ordinances[90]. The letter orders «hele og halve Gylden saa god som rinske Gylden» («whole and half gulden as good as Rhenish gulden»), but was not executed[74][91]; what took effect was the Møntordning of summer 1514, which set the gulden half a carat below the imperial norm[37], repeated by Frederik I in his Møntordning of 1524 (Kongebrev)[75]. The periods differ by which norm was in force: the imperial one for Hans' coinage, which precedes the oldest known Danish prescription (1513); according to Wilcke, probably no ordinance of Hans was issued in Denmark[92]; the Danish one from 1514; under Junge the actual norm of the Rhenish gulden, 18⅓ carats[76][93]; at Flensborg in 1546–1547 again 71⅓ pieces to the mark[77][78]; from 1563 the imperial one again, under the edict of 1559; the ducal lines strike from 1619[94][95] with no ordinance or fineness attested in the sources, so the same imperial norm can be taken as their benchmark; Christian IV's war coinage of 1625–1632 is governed by no published ordinance, its parameters appear from the mint accounts[96].

By function the gulden was a pay coin: Hans had it struck for the German mercenaries in the war against Sten Sture[97], Christian IV for the Thirty Years' War[98]. In the minting table of the Forordning of 8 September 1602 gold is represented by the Portugaløser, the Ungersk Gylden and gold Klippinge, not by the Rhinsk Gylden[99]; the later coinage of crown and dukes stands outside any Danish prescription.

Summary By weight and fineness the lightest and lowest of the contemporary Danish gold standards: about a quarter of the Nobel by weight (Nobelfod, ~14.6 g), and 17¾–18½ carats fine (.740–.771) against .979 for the Nobel and .986 for the Ungersk Gylden (Dukatfod). The gulden comes close to the Ungersk Gylden in weight (~3.25 against ~3.49 g) but carries 26–30 % less fine gold (as computed from 2.42–2.53 against 3.44 g). Documents drawn up in Denmark name the Rhenish gulden by 1406 at the latest, long before its own coinage[154]. Struck alongside other gold: until 1532 beside the Nobel, which the same Møntordning of 1514 regulates; from 1531 beside the Ungersk Gylden; in 1563–1564 and 1584 beside the Guldkrone as well (Frederik-II-Guldkrone-fod). After 1632 the Danish kings strike no more Rhenish gulden; Danish gold thereafter follows the ducat (Dukatfod) and the tariff Guldkrone (Guldkrone-Fuß, from 1618).

The first comprehensive coinage legislation of the Danish-Norwegian realm covering both metals and both kingdoms, established through the Christian II Lovkompleks of 1514-1515[155] — a four-part act: Møntordning af Sommeren 1514 («Dienis Malmø Brev», after mintmaster Diens (Hans) Blicher), Norge ordinance of 3 August 1514 (extension to Norway under the Kalmar Union), Kvittering Paasketid 1515 (compliance receipt from the Rigsråd), Sjælland åbent Brev of 24 August 1515 (Sjælland-specific renewal). On the silver side the Lovkompleks defines the Sølvgylden-Skilling-Hvid triad — 8½ Sølvgylden per Cologne mark rough at 14 Lod (875 ‰), 27.51 g rough / 24.07 g fine per piece; Skilling 96/M at 5¾ Lod and Hvid 222/M at 4½ Lod as Scheidemünzen[155]. Three periods: A1 (1514-1517) Lovkompleks coinage with Sølvgylden 1516+1518 from Malmø (mintmaster Diens Blicher) — preceding 1513 acts (22 July 1513 Haandfæstning + 1 August 1513 Dynes Blicher beskikkelsesbrev) are PRECURSOR, NOT part of the Lovkompleks; A2 (1518-1522) Klipping debasement («ydel Kobber», Wilcke 7-2 p. 157) — 18-Penning and 1-Skilling Klippinge as emergency coinage in the run-up to the deposition; A3 (1523) final consolidation with mass Sølvgylden coinage at Malmø on the eve of the flight of 13 April 1523 (Wilcke 7-2 p. 156 verbatim «meget almindelig»). The standard ends in 1524 with Frederik I's Møntordning of 25 February 1524 (separate Fuß 8-Gylden-Fod), which tightens the Sølvgylden count to 8/M.

Frederik I's coinage-standard norm after the deposition of Christian II, established by the Møntordning of 25 February 1524 (Copenhagen, Wilcke 1950 ch. 2 pp. 183–187)[156]. Royal decree verbatim: «Vi Fr. ... at slaa, i Guld: Nobler (den vegne Mark skal holde 23½ Karat og skraade 16 Stkr.), Rinske Gylden (18 Karat, 72 Stkr.) ...» — and on the silver side: eight Sølvgylden per Cologne mark rough at 14 Lod (.875), 29.232 g rough / 25.578 g fine per piece, Marken-fin mf 9.143. Compared with Christian II's Lovkompleks of 1514, the Sølvgylden count is tightened from 8½/M to 8/M — Frederik I raises the per-piece weight by 6.3 % at unchanged 14-Lod fineness. Innovation: Lybsk-aligned 24-ß Mark subdivision (vs Christian II's 32-ß tariff) — Wilcke 7-2 p. 193 verbatim «Mynten ville udgøre eens og liig ved vores Mynt (o: Hertugdømmets) og udi de Vendiske Stæder». Full sub-Mark apparatus: 2 Mark Danske / 1 Mark / ½ Mark / 4 ß / 4-Hvid ß / Hvid. Separate act of 26 February 1524 establishes the 14-Penning piece («14 %») as Klipping redemption coin — Christian II's Klippinge are exchanged for 14-Penning pieces. Main period 1524-1531; final consolidation 1532 (Galster 47-52 Sølvgylden series). Standard ends with Frederik I's death 10 April 1533; the Christian III interregnum 1534-1540 runs under 8-Daler-Fod Period 0, consolidated de jure by Christian III's Møntordning of 1541 (14½ Lod, 8 Daler/M).

Where the sources diverge on the fineness
Hede gives the Glückstadt and Rendsborg ducats the same fineness as the Copenhagen and Christiania lines — 0.979 — on all eight types for which he prints a fineness at all[165]. Krause lists the same pieces in his Schleswig-Holstein volume under Glückstadt and gives them 0.986, the fineness of the imperial ordinance of 1559, while his Danish and Norwegian volumes carry 0.979[166]. The ducal issues of Gottorp, Tönning, Plön, Sonderburg, Norburg and Glücksburg appear only in the Schleswig-Holstein volume and carry 0.986 with no Danish counterpart. The line therefore runs between the catalogue volumes, not between Holstein and Schleswig. On the precision of the two figures: the 0.986 stands there throughout beside a rough weight of 3.5 g — computed, 233.856 / 3.5 = 66.8 pieces per mark, not 67; Hede's 3.490 g yields 67.01.
Denominations and accounting links 1 Dukat — called Ungersk Gylden until 1629 — is the main denomination across all periods; ½, ¼, 2, 3, 4, 5 and 10 ducat pieces were struck beside it.
1 Portugaløser = 10 ducats: not a standard of its own but the tenfold multiple of this one[167], with half, quarter and double pieces. The tenfold relation lies in the fine gold, not in the rough weight: the ordinance of 1602 gives the Portugaløser 6¾ pieces per mark (34.645 g rough) against 6.7 for ten ducats — lighter in weight yet higher in fineness, 23½ against 23⅓ carats, so that computed, 33.924 / 3.393 = 9.997[99].
Lovbestemt Værdi 1602: Portugaløser 17 daler, Ungersk Gylden 1⅝ daler; the Forordning of 20 November 1604 lowers these to 16 and 1 9/16 and leaves weight and fineness untouched[168]. The tariff thus stands at 10.46 and 10.24 where the metal stands at 9.997 — tariff and weight standard move separately.
The standard has no fixed silver equivalent; outside the set rates the coin was taken by metal content[169].

Dukatfod — the Danish-Norwegian form of the ducat standard: 67 pieces per rough Cologne mark at 23 carats 8 grains. Metrically it is the German Reichsdukatenfuß; legally it is not, since the Augsburg Imperial Coinage Ordinance of 1559 did not bind the kingdom.[158] Adopted without an instrument. The earliest Danish piece on this standard is Frederik I's Ungersk Gylden of 1531.[159] No Danish act stands behind it: the Lovkompleks of 1514 governs Nobel and Rhinsk Gylden, Frederik I's ordinance of 1524 the Nobel at 23 carats — both a different gold. Only the Forordning of 8 September 1602 names this coin in Danish law, and it lowers the fineness to 23⅓ carats for the Ungersk Gylden and 23½ for the Portugaløser.[99] The seventy-one years before that are minting practice attested by coins, not by ordinance. Role. Prestige and trade gold beside the silver territorial currency. The Danish crown never struck it as everyday money but for long-distance commerce and for gift and display; like the Hungarian and Venetian pieces of the same standard that circulated with it, it was taken by metal content.[169] From 1714 the Courantdukatenfuß runs beside it as a distinct, tariff-bearing Danish gold line: the Dukatfod stays the international gold, the Courantdukat the domestic one. The Portugaløser is not a standard of its own but the tenfold multiple of this one: in every period its fine content equals ten contemporary Danish ducats. Its model is the Portuguese português of Manuel I at ten cruzados, which Hamburg imitated between 1553 and 1560;[170] following the Hamburg pattern, Queen Sophie had it struck at Haderslev from 1591 to 1593. The names Ungersk Gylden and Portugaløser hold until 1629, after which the coin is called Dukat. Periods. What separates them is a legal act each time: the Forordning of 8 September 1602 lowers the fineness and closes the first stretch;[99] with the Danish-Norwegian Courant union of 1726 the standard enters its last stretch, which runs to the final striking. End. Striking on this standard continued to 1802 — the latest recorded striking years are 1791-1792, 1794 and 1802 (as computed from the recorded striking years). The Forordning of 5 January 1813 re-bases the territorial currency on the Rigsbankdaler, but its §§1-16 do not cover the gold coins;[171] Danish gold is superseded only by the Scandinavian Monetary Union of 1873 and the Krone.[172]

Period of validity: 1541 → 1555 · 14 yearsSilver
The first systematic coinage-standard norm of the Danish-Norwegian realm after the Reformation, formally codified by the Møntordning of 20 September 1541 of Christian III[174], but metrically already de facto established by Reynold Junge's Joachimsdaler coinage of 1537 in Copenhagen — four years before the formal ordinance. The ordinance sets the mark at 24 per rough Cologne mark at 14½ Lod (906¼ ‰), i.e. eight daler of three marks each; the daler itself as a coin appears only in the ordinance of 27 September 1544[175]. This corresponds to Marken-fin mf 8.827 and 26.494 g fine per Daler. Three-tier denomination: full-value Kurantmünze (Mark / 8 Skilling / 4 Skilling) at 14½ Lod; reduced Scheidemünze (1 Skilling) at 4 Lod (250 ‰); billon (Hvid 4 Lod, Penning 2 Lod). The 3.6× fineness drop between 4 Skilling Kurant and Hvid Scheide marks the step from full-value coin to sub-Mark token tariffed above metal value. Period 0 (1534-1540) — de facto establishment: Notmünz cascade of the Grevens Fejde (Aarhus + Roskilde + Stockholm + Ribe Klippinge at 10/8/7 Lod, 5/4/3½ Lod, 3/2½ Lod — typical wartime tier-debasement) plus the 1537 Joachimsdaler 14½-Lod anchor (Copenhagen; Wilcke 1950 ch. 3 p. 242, table: «1534–39 · Jochimsdaler · 8 · 14½ Lod · 906,25») under mintmaster Reynold Junge[176]. Period A1 (1541-1543) — de jure codification: centralised coinage at the Copenhagen Klarekloster under the new mintmaster Povel Fechtel (Junge dismissed after exposure as a fraudster in 1541, Wilcke 7-4 p. 261). Period A2 (1544-1555) — Tillæg debasement: on 27 September 1544 a new ordinance lowers the mark to 13½ Lod; the Gyldengroschen stays at 14½ Lod and 8 per mark[175]. The Norwegian mint at Trondheim followed the same standard from 1546 (Møntordning of 3 August 1514 extended to the Norwegian realm). The standard effectively ends with the Reichsmünzordnung of 1559 and Frederik II's adoption of the imperial 9-Fuß from 1566; Christian-III Dalers circulated alongside Reichsthalers until the 1566 Münzconvention unification. The Flensborg sub-Mark and 14¼ Lod Daler line from 1547 carries a separate coinage standard (8-Daler-Lybsk-Fod) — see there.

Imperial standard following the Augsburg Imperial Mint Decree of 30 May 1566 — binding on all imperial estates. In Holstein-Gottorp it dominated under Adolf I, Johann Adolf and Friedrich III; the Glückstadt and Tönning mints struck Thaler Species, Mark-lübisch sub-units and Schilling petty coins on this standard. English model and lineage
Verbatim from danskmoent.dk: «Navnet Rosenobel stammer fra den engelske mønt Rose noble fra 13-1400-tallet» — the name derives from the English Rose Noble coin of the 13th-14th centuries[197]. Period of validity: 1602 → 1611 · struck 1604-1605Gold
The gold standard of the Danish-Norwegian ordinance of 8 September 1602, in which the face value is stated not in gold but in the silver unit of account, the daler. The ordinance sets a separate carat and a separate count per mark for each denomination; they converge only through the fine mark, which yields 115½ daler[168]. The standard therefore stands beside the Dukatfod of the same ordinance rather than inside it: there the Portugaløser and Ungersk Gylden come out at a round 9.996 and 0.999 ducats, while the daler klippen give 2.393 · 3.586 · 4.877 — they count in a different unit. The face value was set by law («Lovbestemt Værdi» in the ordinance's own table) and tracked the market ratio of gold to silver: when that ratio moved, the rates were reset on 20 November 1604 — the Portugaløser from 17 to 16 daler, the Ungersk Gylden from 1⅝ to 1 9/16, while the 4-daler klippe kept its face value and was re-cut instead[168]. This gold was not circulation money: it served representation and military pay[199].

The ordinance's minting formulas
The ordinance of 8 September 1602 sets pieces-per-mark and carat separately for each denomination: 8 daler 13¼ pieces at 22½ carats · 6 daler 17¾ at 22⅙ · 4 daler 24 at 20. The 3 daler is not in the ordinance; the mint accounts record it struck «udmøntet som halv 6 Daler Klipping»[200]. On 20 November 1604 the 4-daler klippe is re-cut to 24½ pieces at 20⅓ carats — its face value of 4 daler unchanged[168].
The face value counts in silver
The «daler» struck on these pieces is the silver unit of account, not a gold denomination: at a ratio of 12.8:1 against the daler of 25.983 g fine, the 4-daler klippe answers to 4.000 silver daler and the 6-daler to 5.995. Against the ducat there is no round relation at all (2.393 · 3.586 · 4.877) — unlike the Portugaløser and Ungersk Gylden of the same ordinance, which come out at 9.996 and 0.999 ducats. Wilcke computes the ratio himself: «Sammenholdes Daleren derimod med 4 Daler Guldklipping af Finvægt 8,119 gr., bliver Tallet 12,80»[201].
Place in the monetary system
Not circulation money. Wilcke on the gold of this ordinance: «De Guldmønter, der fremkom, kunde ikke spille nogen virkelig Rolle for Handelssamkvemmet. Deres Forskelligartethed og indbyrdes afvigende Forholdstal til Sølv viser ogsaa, at de snarere var at anse for Skuemønt til festlig Lejlighed end som en konstant Handelsmønt»[199]. For scale he gives the contemporary ratios: England 11.90 · Holland 11.29-11.64 · France 11.88 · southern Germany 12.22-12.24 · Sweden after the 1604 ordinance 13.35.

Christian IV's English-standard experimental gold of 1606-1608. After his 1606 journey to England, Christian IV — following the coin reform of his brother-in-law James I — had Danish «Sovereigns» struck to the English Unite, of approximately the same weight and fineness as the British Unites (22 carats, ca. 10.0 g)[202]. The Christian IV krone was not a trade coin valued by silver content but a fiscal tariff coin: according to Wilcke it held no more silver than the mark pieces it replaced, yet it was issued at 1½ daler in specie[217].

Primary catalogue formula (Hede)[214]: 12 ²⁵⁷⁄₇₀₀ pieces per 13¾-lødig mark = 18.909 g rough at .859 fineness = 16.250 g fine per piece — the piece Hede calls «1 krone», half the Corona Danica. The fractional form follows from the tariff chain of the Forordning af 1. maj 1618: the mark was rated at 20 and the daler at 84 skilling, so the krone as 1½ daler = 126 skilling came to 6⁶⁄₂₀ marks (6³⁄₁₀); Wilcke reckons the silver of those 6³⁄₁₀ marks at 32.400 g, the krone at 32.500 g fine[217]. At the initial tariff, 233.856 g of fine silver yields 7.196 Corona Danica at 1½ specie each, 10.793 speciedaler in all (Hede H105[214]). After the Møntordning af 14. februar 1624 (krone 144, daler 100 skilling[209]) the same mark yields only 10.361 speciedaler (Hede H124[214]).

Wilcke's table of the kronemønt names the pieces as they were called in 1618 and gives each its own weight: krone (Hede 2 krone) 1½ rigsdaler, 37.819 g rough / 32.500 g fine; ½ krone (Hede 1 krone) ¾ rigsdaler, 18.766 / 16.127 g; ortskrone ⅜ rigsdaler, 9.301 / 7.993 g; ⅛ krone ³⁄₁₆ rigsdaler, 4.497 / 3.865 g; ¹⁄₁₆ krone ³⁄₃₂ rigsdaler, 2.238 / 1.923 g. The fine mark was accordingly coined in these sorts to 10.793, 10.875, 10.971, 11.345 and 11.400 rigsdaler[215] — the smaller the piece, the less silver it carried against its tariff.

Under the Forordning af 4. maj 1625 the krone was worth 128 skilling = 2 sletdaler, the half krone 64 skilling = 1 sletdaler = 4 marks of 16 skilling[211]. As no more whole kroner were struck, Wilcke records: «Da Helkroner ikke senere udmøntedes, men derimod Halvkroner, antog disse Navnet Kroner, der ogsaa kaldtes Sletdalere eller „IIII Mark danske“, medens de ældre Helkroner fik Navn af Dobbeltkroner»[219]. The krone of 1618 is therefore the double krone of later catalogues, and its ¾-specie half krone their krone[220]. The new krone was imitated abroad as the «Zwey-Dritler», later the gulden of ⅔ thaler[219].

II. 1670–1726 · Transitional period (proto-Courant in Glückstadt only)
With the Placard of 16 March 1671 («Hebræermønt»), the Crown began debasing the Skilling Danske at Glückstadt below the Hanseatic silver value — over 50 years before the formal Kurantmøntfod of 1726. Outside the royal share, the Hanseatic Marck-Schilling system continued as in Period I, unchanged.

· Holstein-Gottorp, Sonderburg, Norburg-Plön: Hanseatic subdivision unchanged — Gottorp continues to strike ½ Thaler, ⅔ Thaler, 1/16 Thaler at full Hanseatic silver until 1721

III. 1726–1788 · Kurantmøntfod era (for Speciedaler accounting)
The Forordninger of 15/31 July 1726 created the new Rigsdaler Courant (RCour) at the 11⅓-Th-Fuß. Skilling-Danske subdivisions for Holstein formally migrate into the 11⅓-Fuß (see there). For 9¼-Fuß Speciedaler accounting, the Hanseatic Marck-Lübisch system as in Period I continues unchanged.
· Crown convention: 1 Speciedaler = 1¼ Rigsdaler Courant = 120 Skilling Danske (arithmetically — see 11⅓-Fuß)
· 120 × 0.21494 = 25.79 g fine vs. 25.28 g in the Speciedaler — the ~2 % gap was called Opgæld and showed on the exchange market between Specie and Courant
· Placard of 3 December 1794: Opgæld abolished; 5:4 became the sole legal exchange ratio; RCour now sat ~2 % below silver value (seigniorage gain for the Crown)

Silver tariff coin of the Danish-Norwegian composite state. In 1771, under Christian VII, the Royal Mint at Altona opened as the first Holstein-local Kurantmøntfod mint. The arithmetic identities of Period I remain unchanged — Holstein now strikes its own subdivisions in-territory, alongside Copenhagen and Kongsberg (Norway).

III. 1788 · Schleswig-Holstein reform (Holstein leaves the 11⅓-Fuß)
With the Reform of 7 July 1788, Holstein binds its subdivisions to the Speciedaler (see 9¼-Fuß, Period IV): 1 Thaler Species = 60 Schilling S.-H. Courant = 120 Sechslinge. This ends the Skilling-Danske subdivision in the Holstein share; the last Holstein 11⅓-Fuß strike is the Altona Rigsdaler Courant of 1788. In Denmark and Norway the Kurantmøntfod stays in use.

CourantdukatenfußKurantdukatfod · 12-Mark-Goldstück 75 Kurantdukat / rough Cologne mark / 21 Karat
Period of validity: 1714 → 1796 · 82 yearsGold
A specifically Danish tariff-bearing gold line in two metric periods. Period I — Frederik IV (1714–1717): first issue as a war-finance measure in the Great Northern War; 81½ pieces per rough Cologne Mark, 21 carats (.875), 2.511 g fine gold; tariff 12 Mark Kurant = 2 Rigsdaler Courant. The Forordning of 9 July 1727 reduces the tariff to 11 Mark («reducerede Kurant-Dukater»); the Forordning of 4 July 1757 calls the old pieces in for melting.[241] Period II — Frederik V reform (1757–1796): in June 1757 a new standard of 75 pieces per Mark, 3.118 g rough, 2.72832 g fine; the 12-Mark tariff is restored. Mass coinage 1757–1765 at Copenhagen: ≈ 3,030,742 pieces (Finanzkollegium 1791) — the largest gold-coin production in Danish history (≈ 8.3 t fine gold).[241] Christian-VII-birthday strike on 27 January 1771 (131 pieces, Copenhagen). Plakat of 2 December 1782 permits anyone to deliver gold freely for Kurantdukat coinage («gold to be coined at 75 pieces per rough Mark of 21 carats»)[242] — the main period 1781–1796 in Altona + Copenhagen, ≈ 237,000 pieces. The plakat is revoked in 1795; the final issue is 1796 in Altona (≈ 30,700 pieces with 1785 dies).[241] The Statsbankerot of 5 January 1813 reforms only the silver Courant line[171]; the Kurantdukat retains legal-tender status at the 12-Mark tariff until the Scandinavian Monetary Union of 1873.

Summary · 82 years of new coinage · circulation to ~1833, tariff legal-tender to 1873 The Courantdukatenfuß is the specifically Danish answer to the problem of a domesticated gold currency alongside the Reichsdukat: a tariffed, lower-fineness (.875) ducat at the fixed 12-Mark value, set against the higher-fineness (.986) free-floating Reichsdukat. Throughout Period II (1757–1796) it is the «almindeligst gangbare Guldmønt» in Denmark[241] — the Crown's dominant gold currency. In the duchies of Schleswig-Holstein it serves from 1771 onward as the Altona mint's territorial gold supply.[30] No formal demonetisation ordinance — superseded as the official foot by the Rigsbankdaler in 1813, gone from domestic circulation by ~1833[241], yet legally replaced only in 1873 by the Krone of the Scandinavian Monetary Union.

Period of validity: 1771 → 1870 · 99 yearsGold
Pistole gold line of the North-German 5-thaler tariff tradition (modelled on the Wilhelm d'or of 1737 and Friedrich d'or of 1741[183]). Introduced in Denmark in 1771 under Christian VII at the newly opened Altona mint as the Christian d'or, on the French Louis-d'or model[189] — at the same standard as the reduced Prussian Friedrich d'or (130/144 fineness ≈ 903 ‰, 6.03 g fine gold, 5-Speciedaler tariff = 12 Mark Dansk-share). The line continues without interruption under five rulers: Christian VII (1771–1808), Frederik VI (1808–1839, from 1808 as the Frederik d'or — kept on through the Statsbankerot of 1813), Christian VIII (1839–1848), Frederik VII (1848–1863), Christian IX (1863–1870, last issue 1870). Used «mest til internationale betalinger»[189] — a foreign-trade coin, not domestic legal tender. End: the Danish Møntloven af 23. maj 1873 (Scandinavian Monetary Union) replaces the Speciedaler / d'or with the Krone.

Period of validity: 1813 → 1875 · 62 yearsSilver
18½-Thaler-Fuß · Rigsbankdaler / Rigsdaler Rigsmønt — Friedrich VI's emergency reform after the Danish state bankruptcy. The Forordning of 5 January 1813 sets the new principal standard at 18½ Rigsbankdaler per fine Cologne mark (233.856 g) — fine weight ≈ 12.64 g per daler. §10 of the Forordning explicitly anchors 1 Speciedaler = 2 Rigsbankdaler, a clean ½-share link to the 9¼-Thaler-Fuß. In 1854 the coin is renamed Rigsdaler Rigsmønt without any change of standard; in 1873–1875 the Krone of the Scandinavian Monetary Union (Sweden, Norway, Denmark) supersedes the Rigsdaler. The main carrier of the Danish money supply in the second half of the 19th century. In Schleswig-Holstein it ran in parallel with the 9¼-Thaler-Fuß as a lesser actual sub-standard until the Prussian annexation of 24 August 1866.

Krone-fodSkandinavisk Møntunion 2480 Krone / fine kilogram / 900 ‰
Period of validity: 1873 → 1914 · 41 yearsGold · Silver · Bronze
Krone-fod — common principal standard of the Danish-Swedish-Norwegian krone, established by the Danish Møntloven af 23. maj 1873 (Lov nr. 66) and the Scandinavian Monetary Convention of 5 May 1873 (Stockholm).[246][247] Effective from 1 January 1875; Norway accedes in 1875. Denmark had previously rejected joining the Latin Monetary Union (1865) because its bimetallic gold-silver linkage was «both practically impossible and economically irrational».[248]
Daily circulation vs. reserve role: the everyday coins were the silver and øre subsidiary pieces; the gold Hauptkurant primarily served as a foreign-payment reserve and as bank backing. The three Nordic countries struck every denomination to uniform size, weight and fineness with country-specific motifs — all series circulated freely in all three countries until 1914.[247] Convertibility of banknotes into gold coins suspended on 2 August 1914 by Danmarks Nationalbank (outbreak of war).[249] The Scandinavian Monetary Union remained formally in force for coinage until 1924.

Summary · 41 years of the Krone-fod The Danish Krone-fod is the shortest-lived regular principal standard in the monetary history covered here — barely four decades of working gold circulation with a fully integrated silver and bronze subsidiary structure. Structural feature: the graded fineness ladder .900 Au → .800 Ag → .600 / .400 Ag → bronze is the longest-lived innovation of the Møntloven — after the 1914 gold suspension, Denmark continues to mint the subsidiary denominations (the 25 øre transitions from silver to cupro-nickel in 1920–1922, the 10 øre follows; the bronze øre are withdrawn from circulation in 1973).[238] The Scandinavian Monetary Union remained economically successful until the outbreak of war in 1914 effectively ended the unified gold parity; thereafter the three Nordic kroner diverged, leading in 1924 to the formal dissolution of the monetary union.[247] Denmark returned to the gold standard in 1927 at the same gold content as in 1913, and abandoned it permanently in 1931.[249]
