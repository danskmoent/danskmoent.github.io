16-Nobelfod 16 Nobel / rough Cologne mark / 23½ → 23 → 23½ Karat Danish-Norwegian gold standard 1496–1532. King Hans strikes the first Danish Nobel at Malmø and Copenhagen in 1496[1] — the first dated gold coin of Denmark[2]. Per Galster the model is not the English Noble but the Dutch «groote gouden reaal» of 1487, which the Danish piece follows in design and weight, though at a slightly lower fineness[3]. These are prestige coins — not circulation money but display and military-pay gold, struck in connection with the armament against Sweden and markedly heavier than the contemporary Goldgulden — the Rhinsk Gylden (72-Guldgyldenfod) and the Ungersk Gylden (67-Dukatfod)[3]. The Nobel receives its governing specification — 16 pieces from the Cologne mark at 23½ carats — de jure in the Christian II Møntordning of Summer 1514 at Malmø, which codifies the heavy gold piece struck de facto since 1496[4]; the fineness of the pre-1514 Hans Nobler is not independently attested. The lower fineness of the Frederik I ordinance of 1524 is borne out by no surviving piece — the dated 1532 Nobler carry the same higher fineness as the 1514 specification, and the sources give no single Nobel fineness overall[5]. Across three rulers the line stays a series of rare individual issues — Hans 1496 and 1502, Christian II 1516 and 1518, Frederik I 1532 — struck at Malmø and Copenhagen — for the Hans and Frederik I issues Galster leaves the mint undecided between the two — and, undated, at Ribe[1]. With the death of Frederik I in 1533 the coinage ends; Christian III (1534–1559) does not continue the heavy Nobel line; his gold coinage stays with the lighter Goldgulden — Rhinsk Gylden and Ungersk Gylden[1].

72-GuldgyldenfodRhinskgyldenfod 72 Guldgylden / rough Cologne mark / 18 → 18½ Karat The Danish royal-mint variant of the Rhenish-Gulden tradition: the Rhenish gold gulden was the leading gold trade and accounting coin of the Holy Roman Empire[7], so the Danish crown struck its own to that standard as a trade coin for the empire-wide market. The fineness was fixed de jure at 18 carats (.750), 72 pieces per rough Cologne mark, by the Christian II Møntordning (Summer 1514) and the Frederik I ordinance of 25 February 1524[8][9]. The imperial norm itself had until then stood on the form the four Rhenish electors adopted in 1490 — 71⅓ pieces to the mark at 18½ carats[10] — which Karl V upheld in his ordinance of 28 July 1551[11]; only Emperor Ferdinand I's edict of 19 August 1559 moved it to 72 pieces to the 18½-carat mark[12], after which Frederik II struck at that same fineness from 1563. The sections divide by which ordinance was in force. The coinage under Hans (~1496-1510) precedes every Danish gold ordinance and is measured against the imperial norm alone; the German assayers found it in 1551 «kun ca. 3/4 Karat» below that, i.e. at 17¾ carats[13], and Wilcke assigns this gold to the pay of the German mercenaries in the war against Sten Sture[14]. From 1514 the Danish specification of 18 carats governs, under which Frederik I[15] and Christian III[16] strike until 1547 — their pieces of the 1530s holding 18⅓ carats, above the specification and on the output customary within the Empire. From 1563 Frederik II strikes at 18½ carats under the imperial edict of 1559[17]. For the resumption under Christian IV in 1625-1632, during the Imperial-Danish War, no ordinance survives: the 18¼ carats (.76) are a finding on the surviving pieces[17], and the output relations of 1559 continued to hold[12]. Alongside the royal series a second, ducal one runs from 1619 to 1664: Gottorp and the Sonderburg cadet branches struck the Goldgulden on their own account[18] — without an ordinance, and with no fineness recorded for any of the pieces. By function a German-oriented market coin, not tariff money — with no fixed tariff link to the Speciedaler or the Guldkrone.

Summary · 135 years of the Rhenish-Gulden lineage in the Danish royal mint Rhinskgyldenfod is a standalone coinage-standard family of the Rhenish-Gulden tradition, parallel to the Reichsdukatenfuß without tariff binding. Hans' coinage of ~1497 at Malmø precedes every Danish ordinance; the Møntordninger of 1514 and 1524 then set the fineness at 18 carats, and the imperial coinage edict of 1559 at 18½. What the pieces actually held departs from that in both directions: Christian III's coinage of 1534-1536 stands at 18⅓ carats, above the Danish specification and on the customary output within the Empire, while Christian IV's of 1625-1632 stands at 18¼, below it — with no ordinance to cover the reduction. With the end of the main phase in 1632 and Christian IV's pivot to the silver Krone-Mønt (Hebræermønt 1644, Glückstadt 10½-Krone-Fuß 1671) the line effectively ends — Frederik III does not continue it.

Where the sources diverge on the fineness
Hede gives the Glückstadt and Rendsborg ducats the same fineness as the Copenhagen and Christiania lines — 0.979 — on all eight types for which he prints a fineness at all[36]. Krause lists the same pieces in his Schleswig-Holstein volume under Glückstadt and gives them 0.986, the fineness of the imperial ordinance of 1559, while his Danish and Norwegian volumes carry 0.979[37]. The ducal issues of Gottorp, Tönning, Plön, Sonderburg, Norburg and Glücksburg appear only in the Schleswig-Holstein volume and carry 0.986 with no Danish counterpart. The line therefore runs between the catalogue volumes, not between Holstein and Schleswig. On the precision of the two figures: the 0.986 stands there throughout beside a rough weight of 3.5 g — computed, 233.856 / 3.5 = 66.8 pieces per mark, not 67; Hede's 3.490 g yields 67.01.
Denominations and accounting links 1 Dukat — called Ungersk Gylden until 1629 — is the main denomination across all phases; ½, ¼, 2, 3, 4, 5 and 10 ducat pieces were struck beside it.
1 Portugaløser = 10 ducats: not a standard of its own but the tenfold multiple of this one[38], with half, quarter and double pieces. The tenfold relation lies in the fine gold, not in the rough weight: the ordinance of 1602 gives the Portugaløser 6¾ pieces per mark (34.645 g rough) against 6.7 for ten ducats — lighter in weight yet higher in fineness, 23½ against 23⅓ carats, so that computed, 33.924 / 3.393 = 9.997[32].
Lovbestemt Værdi 1602: Portugaløser 17 daler, Ungersk Gylden 1⅝ daler; the Forordning of 20 November 1604 lowers these to 16 and 1 9/16 and leaves weight and fineness untouched[39]. The tariff thus stands at 10.46 and 10.24 where the metal stands at 9.997 — tariff and weight standard move separately.
The standard has no fixed silver equivalent; outside the set rates the coin was taken by metal content[40].

Dukatfod — the Danish-Norwegian form of the ducat standard: 67 pieces per rough Cologne mark at 23 carats 8 grains. Metrically it is the German Reichsdukatenfuß; legally it is not, since the Augsburg Imperial Coinage Ordinance of 1559 did not bind the kingdom.[28] Adopted without an instrument. The earliest Danish piece on this standard is Frederik I's Ungersk Gylden of 1531.[29] No Danish act stands behind it: the Lovkompleks of 1514 governs Nobler and Rhinsk Gylden, Frederik I's ordinance of 1524 the Nobel at 23 carats — both a different gold. Only the Forordning of 8 September 1602 names this coin in Danish law, and it lowers the fineness to 23⅓ carats for the Ungersk Gylden and 23½ for the Portugaløser.[32] The seventy-one years before that are minting practice attested by coins, not by ordinance. Role. Prestige and trade gold beside the silver territorial currency. The Danish crown never struck it as everyday money but for long-distance commerce and for gift and display; like the Hungarian and Venetian pieces of the same standard that circulated with it, it was taken by metal content.[40] From 1714 the Courantdukatenfuß runs beside it as a distinct, tariff-bearing Danish gold line: the Dukatfod stays the international gold, the Courantdukat the domestic one. The Portugaløser is not a standard of its own but the tenfold multiple of this one: in every period its fine content equals ten contemporary Danish ducats. Its model is the Portuguese português of Manuel I at ten cruzados, which Hamburg imitated between 1553 and 1560;[41] following the Hamburg pattern, Queen Sophie had it struck at Haderslev from 1591 to 1593. The names Ungersk Gylden and Portugaløser hold until 1629, after which the coin is called Dukat. Phases. What separates them is a legal act each time: the Forordning of 8 September 1602 lowers the fineness and closes the first stretch;[32] with the Danish-Norwegian Courant union of 1726 the standard enters its last stretch, which runs to the final striking. End. Striking on this standard continued to 1802 — the latest recorded striking years are 1791-1792, 1794 and 1802 (as computed from the recorded striking years). The Forordning of 5 January 1813 re-bases the territorial currency on the Rigsbankdaler, but its §§1-16 do not cover the gold coins;[42] Danish gold is superseded only by the Scandinavian Monetary Union of 1873 and the Krone.[43]

Three parallel gold types (contextualisation)
In 1563-1564 Frederik II strikes three parallel gold types as Klippinge at Copenhagen[45]: Ungersk Gylden (Hede f2h1 / f2h4, .986 / 3.49 g — Reichsdukatenfuß standard, reverse inscription «I DOK KATE»); Rhinsk Gylden (Hede f2h3 / f2h6, .77 / 3.248 g — Rhinskgyldenfod, inscription «I GOLT GILDEN»); Guldkrone from Kroneguld (Hede f2h2 / f2h5, .934 / 3.341 g — this standard, inscription «I GOLT KRONE»). The three lines serve three market roles: imperial trade reserve (Ungersk), empire-wide trade (Rhinsk), and Danish-royal wartime financing (Krone).
Mintmaster triad
Wilcke 1950 Kap. 6 documents three responsible mintmasters for the Frederik II Klipping period[46]: Hans de Wüllers (goldsmith, 1563), Hans Köppelin (mint-master, 1564), Laurits Hammer (mint-master, Bremerholm, 1565). Coinage at the Bremerholm-Copenhagen royal mint. The Klipping format (square / rectangular die-cut coins) is the typical Renaissance emergency-money form — faster to produce than round die-struck pieces, optimised for wartime volume.
Relation to the later Guldkrone tariff coin (Christian IV / Frederik III)
Standalone coinage-standard lineage. The Frederik-II-Guldkrone-fod (1563-1564, .934 / 3.341 g rough / 3.120 g fine) shares the name but is metrically not identical to the later Guldkrone tariff coin family (Guldkrone-Fuß: Christian IV 1618-1648 + Frederik III 1655-1668, .917-.934 / 5.996 g rough / 5.498 g fine, tariff 3½ Speciedaler). 34-year documented-window hiatus between 1584 and 1619; metric discontinuity (small piece under Frederik II vs large tariff piece under Christian IV); functional discontinuity (emergency coin under Frederik II vs tariff Hauptkurant under Frederik III). Therefore classified as a standalone coinage standard, not a phase under Guldkrone-Fuß.

Frederik II strikes a unique Rosenobel predecessor at Frederiksborg in 1584 at the classical English brutto of 7.69 g (Hede 7B / Schou 2 / Sieg 29) — probably closer to the English original 7.78 g (Edward IV 1465 Rose Noble). Function throughout: a prestige gold issue for mercenary pay and diplomatic representation, not daily circulation. Tariff vs. Speciedaler computed ≈ 3-4 Specie (no period-attested formal tariff). Verbatim from danskmoent.dk: «Navnet Rosenobel stammer fra den engelske mønt Rose noble fra 13-1400-tallet» — the name derives from the English Rose Noble coin of the 13th-14th centuries[52]. The English Rose Noble (Edward IV 1465, 7.78 g, 10s = half-pound, .995 fineness) is the late-medieval model; the Danish Rosenobel coinage is a late adaptation in the Renaissance context, mediated through the Øresund Toll (introduced by Hans in 1497). Frederik II's single 1584 piece (7.69 g) still follows the classical 7.78-g standard; Christian IV's mass coinage 1611-1629 (8.994 g at .833) extends the standard in the spirit of Danish Renaissance mint practice.
Economic background — Kalmar War and mercenary pay
Christian IV's main coinage period 1611-1613 coincides exactly with the Kalmar War (1611-1613)[54]: «King Christian IV attacked Sweden in the 1611-13 Kalmar War but failed to accomplish his main objective of forcing Sweden to return to the union with Denmark. The war led to no territorial changes, but Sweden was forced to pay a war indemnity of 1 million silver riksdaler to Denmark». The prestige gold issue served mercenary pay and diplomatic representation. The follow-up coinage 1627 + 1629 falls within the start of Danish involvement in the Thirty Years' War (Imperial-Danish War 1625-1629) — the same function under a different conflict.

8-Daler-FodDalerfod (Wilcke 1950 verbatim) 8 Daler / rough Cologne mark / 14½ Lod The first systematic coinage-standard norm of the Danish-Norwegian realm after the Reformation, formally codified by the Møntordning of 20 September 1541 of Christian III[77], but metrically already de facto established by Reynold Junge's Joachimsdaler coinage of 1537 in Copenhagen — four years before the formal ordinance. The ordinance sets the mark at 24 per rough Cologne mark at 14½ Lod (906¼ ‰), i.e. eight daler of three marks each; the daler itself as a coin appears only in the ordinance of 27 September 1544[78]. This corresponds to Marken-fin mf 8.827 and 26.494 g fine per Daler. Three-tier denomination: full-value Kurantmünze (Mark / 8 Skilling / 4 Skilling) at 14½ Lod; reduced Scheidemünze (1 Skilling) at 4 Lod (250 ‰); billon (Hvid 4 Lod, Penning 2 Lod). The 3.6× fineness drop between 4 Skilling Kurant and Hvid Scheide marks the step from full-value coin to sub-Mark token tariffed above metal value. Phase 0 (1534-1540) — de facto establishment: Notmünz cascade of the Grevens Fejde (Aarhus + Roskilde + Stockholm + Ribe Klippinge at 10/8/7 Lod, 5/4/3½ Lod, 3/2½ Lod — typical wartime tier-debasement) plus the 1537 Joachimsdaler 14½-Lod anchor (Copenhagen; Wilcke 1950 ch. 3 p. 242, table: «1534–39 · Jochimsdaler · 8 · 14½ Lod · 906,25») under mintmaster Reynold Junge[79]. Phase A1 (1541-1543) — de jure codification: centralised coinage at the Copenhagen Klarekloster under the new mintmaster Povel Fechtel (Junge dismissed after exposure as a fraudster in 1541, Wilcke 7-4 p. 261). Phase A2 (1544-1555) — Tillæg debasement: on 27 September 1544 a new ordinance lowers the mark to 13½ Lod; the Gyldengroschen stays at 14½ Lod and 8 per mark[78]. The Norwegian mint at Trondheim followed the same standard from 1546 (Møntordning of 3 August 1514 extended to the Norwegian realm). The standard effectively ends with the Reichsmünzordnung of 1559 and Frederik II's adoption of the imperial 9-Fuß from 1566; Christian-III Dalers circulated alongside Reichsthalers until the 1566 Münzconvention unification. The Flensborg sub-Mark and 14¼ Lod Daler line from 1547 carries a separate coinage standard (8-Daler-Lybsk-Fod) — see there.

9-Thaler-FußReichsmüntzfuß 9 Reichsthaler / fine Cologne mark / 14²⁄₉ Loth Imperial standard following the Augsburg Imperial Mint Decree of 30 May 1566 — binding on all imperial estates. In Holstein-Gottorp it dominated under Adolf I, Johann Adolf and Friedrich III; the Glückstadt and Tönning mints struck Thaler Species, Mark-lübisch sub-units and Schilling petty coins on this standard. The Kipper-Wipper crisis (1620–1623) collapsed the unified imperial coinage; from 1622 onward (Friedrich III of Gottorp, Glückstadt) the lighter 9¼-Thaler-Fuß took over as the large-silver standard, and the last Holstein 9-Fuß strike falls in 1625 (Hans Jr. of Sonderburg, 2 Schilling Lübisch). Beyond Holstein the 9-Fuß remained in force as bank-exchange money: Hamburg kept it as the Bank-Mark of the Hamburger Bank, Lübeck as the Speciemark — both until the imperial coinage reform of 1875.

Christian IV's kroner 12 ²⁵⁷⁄₇₀₀ Krone / rough Cologne mark / 13¾ Lod Christian IV's kroner 1618–1625. A tariff coin, introduced by the åbne Brev af 12. juli 1618, whose § 1 set the krone — the coin later catalogues call the double krone or Corona Danica — at 1½ rigsdaler in specie = 126 skilling dansk[89]. Whoever accepted a krone for 1½ daler lost, on Wilcke's reckoning, ca. 16 % (32.5 g against ca. 38.5 g of fine silver), and ca. 20 % with the cost of recoining — the Council of the Realm's own estimate[90]. The Møntordning af 14. februar 1624 set the krone at 144 and the daler at 100 skilling[91]. Following the Rigsraadets Betænkning om Mønten af 15. februar 1625[92], krone minting ended in the summer of 1625; the Forordning om Mønten af 4. maj 1625 set the rigsdaler at 96 and the krone at 128 skilling, i.e. down from 1½ to 1⅓ daler[93][94]. A 19-year hiatus of krone production follows, until the revival in the 10½-Krone-Fuß (Glückstadt) in 1644.[95][96]

The Christian IV krone was not a trade coin valued by silver content but a fiscal tariff coin: according to Wilcke it held no more silver than the mark pieces it replaced, yet it was issued at 1½ daler in specie[99].

Primary catalogue formula (Hede)[96]: 12 ²⁵⁷⁄₇₀₀ pieces per 13¾-lødig mark = 18.909 g rough at .859 fineness = 16.250 g fine per piece — the piece Hede calls «1 krone», half the Corona Danica. The fractional form follows from the tariff chain of the Forordning af 1. maj 1618: the mark was rated at 20 and the daler at 84 skilling, so the krone as 1½ daler = 126 skilling came to 6⁶⁄₂₀ marks (6³⁄₁₀); Wilcke reckons the silver of those 6³⁄₁₀ marks at 32.400 g, the krone at 32.500 g fine[99]. At the initial tariff, 233.856 g of fine silver yields 7.196 Corona Danica at 1½ specie each, 10.793 speciedaler in all (Hede H105[96]). After the Møntordning af 14. februar 1624 (krone 144, daler 100 skilling[91]) the same mark yields only 10.361 speciedaler (Hede H124[96]).

Wilcke's table of the kronemønt names the pieces as they were called in 1618 and gives each its own weight: krone (Hede 2 krone) 1½ rigsdaler, 37.819 g rough / 32.500 g fine; ½ krone (Hede 1 krone) ¾ rigsdaler, 18.766 / 16.127 g; ortskrone ⅜ rigsdaler, 9.301 / 7.993 g; ⅛ krone ³⁄₁₆ rigsdaler, 4.497 / 3.865 g; ¹⁄₁₆ krone ³⁄₃₂ rigsdaler, 2.238 / 1.923 g. The fine mark was accordingly coined in these sorts to 10.793, 10.875, 10.971, 11.345 and 11.400 rigsdaler[97] — the smaller the piece, the less silver it carried against its tariff.

Under the Forordning af 4. maj 1625 the krone was worth 128 skilling = 2 sletdaler, the half krone 64 skilling = 1 sletdaler = 4 marks of 16 skilling[93]. As no more whole kroner were struck, Wilcke records: «Da Helkroner ikke senere udmøntedes, men derimod Halvkroner, antog disse Navnet Kroner, der ogsaa kaldtes Sletdalere eller „IIII Mark danske“, medens de ældre Helkroner fik Navn af Dobbeltkroner»[101]. The krone of 1618 is therefore the double krone of later catalogues, and its ¾-specie half krone their krone[102]. The new krone was imitated abroad as the «Zwey-Dritler», later the gulden of ⅔ thaler[101].

Relation to the older Krone-Mønt: the parallel-running Krone (10½- and 13-Krone-Fuß) remained in account — 1 Krone (silver) = 4 Marck Danske = 60 Kroneskilling = 64 Skilling Danske = ⅔ Rigsdaler Courant = 13.75623 g fine · 1 Kroneskilling = 16/15 Skilling Danske

II. 1771–1788 · The Royal Mint at Altona opens
In 1771, under Christian VII, the Royal Mint at Altona opened as the first Holstein-local Kurantmøntfod mint. The arithmetic identities of Period I remain unchanged — Holstein now strikes its own subdivisions in-territory, alongside Copenhagen and Kongsberg (Norway).
· Holstein-Altona issues: 24 Skilling Danske = ¼ RCour (KM #250, 1772–1788, silver .562, a «Rigsort») · 4 Skilling Dansk (KM #631, 1783) · 2 Skilling Danske (KM #632, 1778–85) · 1 Skilling Dansk copper token (KM #616, 1771) · ½ Skilling Dansk copper token (KM #615, 1771) · 1 Rigsdaler Courant (1788, the last Altona strike)
· Numismatic verification: 24 Sk Dansk KM #250 — silver .562 · 9.17 g rough · 5.15354 g fine → 1 Sk = 0.21473 g fine ✓ (target: 0.21494 g, Δ 0.1 %)

III. 1788 · Schleswig-Holstein reform (Holstein leaves the 11⅓-Fuß)
With the Reform of 7 July 1788, Holstein binds its subdivisions to the Speciedaler (see 9¼-Fuß, Period IV): 1 Thaler Species = 60 Schilling S.-H. Courant = 120 Sechslinge. This ends the Skilling-Danske subdivision in the Holstein share; the last Holstein 11⅓-Fuß strike is the Altona Rigsdaler Courant of 1788. In Denmark and Norway the Kurantmøntfod stays in use.

IV. 1794 · Placard of 3 December 1794 (Denmark/Norway, not Holstein)
· Opgæld market rate abolished; 1 RSp = 1¼ RCour = 5:4 became the sole legal exchange ratio
· The Crown broke silver parity at the Courant's expense: 125 × 20.63435 = 2579.29 g silver vs. 100 × 25.28173 = 2528.17 g — RCour now sat ~2 % below its silver value (seigniorage gain for the Crown)
· The Holstein share, having left in 1788, was no longer affected — the Placard worked only in Denmark and Norway

V. 1813 · Statsbankerot — replaced by the Rigsbankdaler
· Forordning of 5 January 1813: 1 RSp = 2 Rigsbankdaler = 12 Rigsbankmark = 192 Rigsbankskilling · Kurantmøntfod ends Denmark-wide
· Subdivisions migrate into the 18½-Fuß (see there)

Antecedents: as early as 1670–1726, royal Glückstadt was striking 8-Skilling-Danske pieces (KM #82.1 1694, KM #1 1702, KM #3 1711) at the proto-Courant silver content of 0.21494 g per Sk, identical with the later 11⅓-Fuß subdivision — see 9¼-Fuß, Period II.

Summary · 41 years of the Krone-fod The Danish Krone-fod is the shortest-lived regular principal standard in the monetary history covered here — barely four decades of working gold circulation with a fully integrated silver and bronze subsidiary structure. Structural feature: the graded fineness ladder .900 Au → .800 Ag → .600 / .400 Ag → bronze is the longest-lived innovation of the Møntloven — after the 1914 gold suspension, Denmark continues to mint the subsidiary denominations (the 25 øre transitions from silver to cupro-nickel in 1920–1922, the 10 øre follows; the bronze øre are withdrawn from circulation in 1973).[111] The Scandinavian Monetary Union remained economically successful until the outbreak of war in 1914 effectively ended the unified gold parity; thereafter the three Nordic kroner diverged, leading in 1924 to the formal dissolution of the monetary union.[115] Denmark returned to the gold standard in 1927 at the same gold content as in 1913, and abandoned it permanently in 1931.[117]
