Instructie Maximilian juli 1487 — Burgundian Netherlands
16½ Reaal / rough troy mark / 23⅘ Karat
The Instructie of July 1487 amends the instruction to the mint masters under the Ordonnantie of 20 April 1487, which had prescribed the gold Reaal at 17 to the mark[42]: the Reaal is to be struck at 16½ to the mark, the Bourgondische nobel at 33, the Dukaat (Florin) at 66[20]. The mark is the Trooise mark[21] of 244.753 g[22]. The Reaal of 1487 had a fineness of 23⅘ carats (99.17 %) and weighed 14.834 g, of which 14.710 g fine gold[23][24]. The Instructie itself does not state the fineness[20]; the instruction under the Ordonnantie of 20 April 1487 writes «24 carats»[42] — in the Netherlands instructions the usual term for fine gold, as in 1489: «24 carats — le noble Henricus compté pour fin» («the Henricus noble counted as fine»)[43], i.e. gold of the English noble's fineness (≈ .995)[44]. Van Gelder gives 14.83 g and 14.72 g of fine gold[45][46], which corresponds to a slightly higher fineness of ≈ 23 13/16 carats (as computed).
Period I · 1496 → 1513 · Hans · after the model of the Instructie 1487
16½ Nobel / rough troy mark / ?
King Hans begins the coinage of Danish Nobel; according to Galster this is probably connected with the preparations for the Swedish campaign[34]. Wilcke adds the general background: Hans used the coinage to obtain funds for his wars against Sweden and Dithmarschen, and was constantly short of money[47]. No Danish ordinance for the Nobel is known: as Galster notes, «Der er ikke bevaret nogen dansk Møntordning fra Kong Hans» («No Danish Møntordning from King Hans has been preserved»)[4]. The piece count (16½ to the troy mark) is that of the Dutch model[25]; the fineness of the Danish Nobel is not independently attested[33]. The model, the great Dutch gold real of 1487, was a representative coin in the Netherlands («penning van toog en parament», i.e. a coin of pomp and ornament)[48], while gold was then practically not struck there apart from a modest coinage at Dordrecht[49]; the Danish Nobel even take over details of the model: the Guelders cross between the beginning and the end of the legend and the rose in the exergue, the mint mark of Dordrecht[50]. According to Galster, the Nobel of Hans, like those of Christian II, are first and foremost «Pragtmønter» (show coins)[35]. The exact reason for the coinage is unknown; Stack's Bowers considers it likely that the Nobel was struck for the king's personal use as a gift for foreign dignitaries[51].

Møntordning Christian II 1514
16 Nobel / rough Cologne mark / 23½ Karat
The Møntordning Christian II of summer 1514 — part of the Christian II Lovkompleks of 1514–1515[52] — specifies the Nobel: 16 pieces per Cologne mark at 23½ carats[26]; under the act the Nobel is worth 6 gulden: «da er Stykket 6 Gylden» («the piece is worth 6 gulden»)[53]. Against the 1487 Reaal (23⅘ carats[23], 14.834 g) the 1514 Nobel is 3/10 carat lower and ≈ 1.5 % lighter (14.616 g) — as computed.
Period II · 1516 → 1518 · Christian II
16 Nobel / rough Cologne mark / 23½ Karat
Wilcke observes that valuable gold coin appears precisely under wartime conditions: in 1496 and 1516 with the years that followed each, and again in 1532[36]. According to Galster, the Nobel of Christian II of 1516 and 1518 (like those of Hans) are first and foremost «Pragtmønter» (show coins) that owe their origin to the military preparations against Sweden[35]; whether they meet the fineness required by the Møntordning of 1514 is uncertain[38].

Rigsraadets Brev, 25 February 1524
16 Nobel / rough Cologne mark / 23 Karat
The open letter of the Council of the Realm, issued on the elected King Frederik’s behalf to the mintmaster and burgomaster of Malmø Jørgen Kock[54], first orders the silver Klippinge of Christian II to be recoined into round coin[55]; it then allows Kock to strike «fyn nobell guild» («fine, noble gold») — 16 pieces to the weighed mark at 23 carats[27][28], half a carat below the norm of 1514[39]. According to Galster the ordinance had «no practical significance», since it was replaced the same year by a new one[56]. No coins under this prescription are attested: no Nobel of Frederik I from Malmø in 1524 is known, the Ribe mint only began work in the summer of 1524[57] and received the royal letter alone[58], which restores the Nobel to its previous fineness[40].
Møntordning Frederik I 1524 (Kongebrev)
16 Nobel / rough Cologne mark / 23½ Karat
Frederik I’s Møntordning of 1524 was issued in the form of a royal open letter, after 25 February, to the same Jørgen Kock at Malmø, with a like letter to the mintmaster at Ribe[58]. It is the same text as the Council’s letter, in a royal redaction with changes[59]: it orders the coin «over hele Riget» («throughout the whole Realm»)[60] and restores the Nobel’s previous fineness: 23½ carats at 16 pieces[29][40].
Period III · 1524 → 1532 · Frederik I
16 Nobel / rough Cologne mark / 23½ Karat
The mint in the town of Ribe struck coins under Frederik I only in 1524–1526[61]; Ernst assigns the undated Nobel of Ribe to 1524 or 1525 and calls them show coins[62], which he tentatively connects with the signing at Ribe in November 1524 of the Norwegian charter by which the King recognised Norway as a hereditary kingdom[63]. Wilcke takes the Nobel of Ribe to be presumably trial pieces (Prøver), since the means for a regular coinage did not seem to be available at that time[64]. The Nobel of 1532 were struck during the armament against Christian II[65], who, after his flight on 13 April 1523[66], landed in Norway on 5 November 1531[67]. The coinage of 1532–33 under Frederik I was struck from gold and silver requisitioned from all the churches of the realm in 1531; according to the surviving account of these coinages, of gold coin only Nobel were struck[68]. According to Wilcke, these Nobel were exceptionally heavy, on average triple, and were surely meant to be given to the higher commanders[69]. Ernst, who discusses the account of these coinages, takes the number «21» in it to be probably a scribal error (for «61»), in which case these would be ordinary Nobel and one «thick», probably triple, Nobel[70].
