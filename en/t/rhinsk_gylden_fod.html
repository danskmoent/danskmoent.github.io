Phase 0 · 1496 → 1510 · Before the first Danish coinage ordinance · benchmark: Wormser Reichsabschied 1495 — 18½ carats · assayed fineness 17¾ carats
Hans' coinage (r. 1481-1513) precedes every Danish gold ordinance — the Empire had already made the four Rhenish electors' norm binding through the Wormser Reichsabschied 1495[19][10], while Denmark did so only in 1514. The undated Malmø/Copenhagen coinage «formentlig slået omkring 1497» was struck to pay the German mercenaries in the war against Sten Sture[14]; after Hvitfeldt, 150,000 pieces were struck for it[21]. Under the same reign Hans also strikes at Bergen (~1505-1510) — REX NORWEGIE / MONETA NOVA AVREA BERGE, the earliest gold coin struck in Norway[22]. What the king himself understood by the standard is recorded in his Møntanordning of 4 December 1497: «guldmynt på Rhenska gulden slag», 72 pieces to the 18½-carat mark — an order issued for Sweden and never executed[23]. The Nürnberg Valvationstag of 1551 found the surviving type «kun ca. 3/4 Karat» below that norm, i.e. at 17¾ carats[13]; the shortfall in fineness is therefore measured, not decreed, and shows as a deviation.

Phase I · 1514 → 1547 · Møntordning of Summer 1514 · Møntordning of 25 February 1524 — 72 pieces to the rough Cologne Mark at 18 carats · 2.436 g fine per piece
The Møntordning of Christian II of Summer 1514 gives the Rhinsk Gylden its first Danish legal basis — 18 carats at 72 pieces to the mark[8] — and the Møntordning of Frederik I of 25 February 1524 restates that gold grid unchanged[9]. Both set the fineness half a carat below the Rhenish norm. Frederik I (r. 1523-1533) strikes at Malmø in 1527[15], Christian III (r. 1534-1559) at Roskilde in 1536 and at Flensburg in 1546-1547 (1 and 2 Goldgulden)[20]; the Flensburg coinage closes the phase. Running alongside it in the same years is the king's coinage in his ducal capacity — Gottorp, Flensburg, Holstein — on the same specification; the 1536 piece carries both issuers at once. The pieces struck in 1534-1536 hold at .764 not less but more than the Danish specification: they sit at the 18⅓ carats which the Valvationstag of 1551 records as the customary output of the Normalgylden within the Empire — 108 pieces to 1½ marks, for which no ordinance is recorded[24]. Above the Danish specification, yet below the imperial norm of 18½ carats — and it is this shortfall against the imperial norm that Wilcke connects to Emperor Ferdinand I's edict of 19 August 1559, which declared Christian III's and Kong Hans' Guldgylden void[12].

Phase II · 1563 → 1584 · Coinage edict of Emperor Ferdinand I of 19 August 1559 — 72 pieces to the mark at 18½ carats · 2.50367 g fine per piece
No Danish gold ordinance is issued between 1524 and 1563; what governs is the coinage edict of Emperor Ferdinand I of 19 August 1559, which fixes the fineness «endelig … til 72 Stkr. af den 18 1/2 Karat fine Mark» and at the same time voids, at six months' notice, the older Guldgylden of Christian III and Kong Hans[12]. It is on exactly that fineness that Frederik II strikes from 1563 at Copenhagen and in 1584 at Frederiksborg[17] — Danish coinage here follows an imperial, not a Danish, legal act. The occasion for the resumption is the financing of the Northern Seven Years' War, for which Frederik II has three kinds of gold struck at once: Ungersk Gylden, Rhinsk Gylden and Kroneguld[25].

Phase III · 1619 → 1627 · Parallel ducal coinage · Gottorp and the Sonderburg lines · no ordinance anchor, no fineness recorded
In the duchies the Rhenish-standard Goldgulden did not come from the crown but from the ducal lines: Gottorp and the Sonderburg cadet branches struck it between 1619 and 1664[18]. This series is not a sequential period but a concurrent ducal line: it begins six years before Christian IV's coinage, covers it entirely, and runs on in the duchies for thirty-two years after it ends. The Sonderburg dukes held no coinage right at all — the partitioned duchy was a titular one, «das weder ein Münzrecht erhielt noch stehende Truppen unterhalten durfte» — and struck regardless. No fineness is recorded for any of these issues; the standard itself remains the Worms anchor of 72 pieces to the rough Cologne mark[19].

Phase IV · 1625 → 1632 · No ordinance anchor of its own · the coinage edict of 19 August 1559 still governs (18½ carats) · assayed fineness 18¼ carats
Coinage resumes under Christian IV in the years 1625, 1627, 1628, 1632 — the span of the Imperial-Danish War (entry 1625, defeat at Lutter am Barenberge 1626, Peace of Lübeck 1629). No ordinance survives for this coinage: the output relations of the 1559 edict held «helt ind i Christian IV.s Tid»[12]. The fineness reduced to 18¼ carats is a finding on the surviving pieces, not a specification[17]; Wilcke assigns this gold to military pay and records that it was «til Fordel for Kongen — været søgt noget forringet i Gehalt»[13]. The deviation therefore shows in the fine-weight balance. With the end of coinage in 1632 the line closes; Christian IV later turns to the silver Krone-Mønt (Hebræermønt 1644).
