Phase 0 · 1481 → 1510 · Before the first Danish ordinance · benchmark: the electors' norm of 1490 and the Reichsabschied of Worms 1495 — 71⅓ pieces at 18½ carats · assayed fineness 17¾ carats
The coinage of Hans (r. 1481-1513) precedes any Danish gold ordinance. Its benchmark is the norm of the four Rhenish electors of 1490 — 71⅓ pieces from the rough Cologne mark at 18½ carats — made binding throughout the Empire by the Reichsabschied of Worms in 1495[19][20]. The undated gulden from Malmø or København, «formentlig slået omkring 1497» («presumably struck around 1497»), paid the German mercenaries in the war against Sten Sture[43]; according to Hvitfeldt the king had 150,000 struck for the campaign of 1497[45]. Hans also struck the half gulden[24] and at Bergen a gulden reading REX NORWEGIE / MONETA NOVA AVREA BERGE[46]. His Møntanordning for Sweden of 4 December 1497 prescribes «guldmynt på Rhenska gulden slag» («gold coin of the Rhenish gulden kind»), 72 pieces to the 18½-carat mark, but was never executed[47]. The Nürnberg Valvationstag of 1551 found the surviving type «kun ca. 3/4 Karat» («only about ¾ carat») below the norm, i.e. at 17¾ carats[23]; Wilcke counts this gold as soldiers' pay, reduced in fineness «til Fordel for Kongen» («to the King’s advantage»)[48]. A second type, with a star in the small shield at the king's feet — according to Wilcke the Holstein nettle leaf — was assessed by the Valvationstag at 17 carats[49]; no specimen of it seems to survive[50].

Phase I · 1527 → 1536
I.a · 1527 → 1531 · Møntordning of summer 1514 · Kongebrev 1524 — 72 pieces from the rough Cologne mark at 18 carats
The first Danish instrument on the gulden — Christian II's letter of appointment for the mintmaster Dynes Blicher of 1 August 1513 — orders «hele og halve Gylden saa god som rinske Gylden» («whole and half gulden as good as Rhenish gulden»), but was not executed[40]. What took effect was the Møntordning of summer 1514: 72 pieces from the mark at 18 carats, half a carat below the imperial norm[25]; Frederik I repeated it in the royal letter (Kongebrev) of 1524[26]. Under this prescription Frederik I strikes at Malmø in 1527[51] and at Gottorp in 1531[27].

I.b · 1534 → 1536 · Reynold Junge's account 1534–1536 — 72 pieces from the rough Cologne mark at 18⅓ carats
Under Christian III the mintmaster Reynold Junge alloys the gold for the gulden at 18 carats 4 grains (18⅓ carats) and cuts 72 pieces to the mark; by his account they number 24,470 gulden[28]. That is a third of a carat above the Danish prescription and exactly the fineness the Valvationstag of 1551 records as the usual actual outturn of the Rhenish gulden in the Empire[52]. The coinage is struck at Gottorp in 1534 and at Roskilde in 1536[27][53]. The Recess of 1540 sets the rate: the Rhenish gulden no higher than 3 Mark danske, equal to the Joachimsthaler[54].

Phase II · 1546 → 1547
II.a · 1546 → 1546 · Flensborg 1546 · Reynold Junge — 71⅓ pieces from the rough Cologne mark at 18 carats
The Flensborg mint is re-established in 1544; after the death of the mintmaster Jørgen Degener it is run in 1546 by Reynold Junge[55]. The single and double gulden of 1546 are known with his mark, per Hede at 3.278 and 6.556 g at .750 — i.e. 71⅓ pieces to the mark at 18 carats (as computed from the weights)[29]. No ordinance of its own survives for this coinage.

II.b · 1547 → 1547 · Christian III's letter appointing Jørgen Kock, 22 January 1547 — 71⅓ pieces from the rough Cologne mark at 18½ carats
On 22 January 1547 Christian III appoints Jørgen Kock the younger mintmaster at Flensborg[55]; the letter of appointment orders the gulden «efter Kurfyrsternes Gylden» («after the electors’ gulden») — 71⅓ pieces to the mark at 18½ carats[30][19]. A new type is known from the same year, with the Holstein arms on the obverse[31]. The sources give neither weight nor fineness for this coin.

Phase III · 1563 → 1584 · Coinage edict of Emperor Ferdinand I of 19 August 1559 — 72 pieces to the mark at 18½ carats · 2.50367 g fine per piece
The coinage edict of Emperor Ferdinand I of 19 August 1559 finally fixes «72 Stkr. af den 18 1/2 Karat fine Mark» («72 pieces from the mark of 18½ carats fine») and at the same time, with six months' notice, declares the older gulden of Christian III and Hans invalid[32]. There is no new Danish gulden ordinance after 1547; Frederik II strikes from 1563 at København to the edict's fineness, 18½ carats[32][33], and in 1584 at Frederiksborg for Queen Sophie's gold gift set[27]. The metal of 1563 came from a loan of Elector August of Saxony, 65,000 gulden and 26,875 thalers; in the same year 3,254 «Guldgylden Klippinge» («gold gulden klippes») were struck for the troops of the Nordic Seven Years' War[56].

Phase IV · 1619 → 1627 · Ducal lines · Gottorp and the Sonderburg lines as vassals of the Danish crown · no ordinance of their own, fineness not given
Gottorp and the Sonderburg cadet lines struck the Goldgulden to the Rhenish norm from 1619[41]. The dukes had no ordinance of their own, and for none of these coinages do the sources give the fineness; the benchmark is the imperial norm then in force, the edict of 1559 — 72 pieces from the mark at 18½ carats[32].

Phase V · 1625 → 1632 · Christian IV · no ordinance of its own · the coinage edict of 19 August 1559 (18½ carats) remains in force · fineness as found 18¼ carats
Under Christian IV coinage resumes at København: gulden are dated 1625, 1627, 1628 and 1632; according to the accounts some 34,000 pieces at 1¼ Rigsdaler were struck[42][34]. As early as 1625 «var givet Befaling til Udmøntning af et stort Antal Guldgylden til Lønning af Krigsfolket» («orders had been given to strike a large number of gold gulden to pay the soldiery»)[35]; the pieces of 1627 and 1628 are, per Harck, «oplagt slået til anvendelse i forbindelse med trediveårskrigen» («evidently struck for use in connection with the Thirty Years’ War»)[42]. No ordinance for this coinage survives; the relations of the 1559 edict remained valid «helt ind i Christian IV.s Tid» («well into the time of Christian IV»)[32]. The fineness of 18¼ carats is not a prescription but what the pieces show[34][35]; Wilcke counts this gold as soldiers' pay, reduced in fineness «til Fordel for Kongen» («to the King’s advantage»)[48]. The Forordning of 8 September 1602 no longer provides for the Rhenish gulden[44]; the coinage stands outside any Danish prescription.
