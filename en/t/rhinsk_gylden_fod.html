The electors' norm of 1490 was adopted as the imperial norm at the Diet of Worms in 1495 (convened by Maximilian I) and confirmed at Lindau in 1497 and at Freiburg in 1498[79][86][100].
Period I · 1497 → 1510 · King Hans · on the imperial norm (Worms 1495)
71⅓ Goldgulden / rough Cologne mark / 17¾ Karat
No Danish Møntordning survives from the reign of Hans (1481-1513)[4]. Whether Hans issued such ordinances is disputed in the literature: according to Galster, the ordinance of 1513 merely repeats provisions of «older, lost» Danish ordinances[90], while Wilcke holds that probably none was issued in Denmark[92]. The undated gulden from Malmø or København, «formentlig slået omkring 1497» («presumably struck around 1497»), paid the German mercenaries in the war against Sten Sture[97]. His Møntanordning for Sweden of 4 December 1497 prescribes «guldmynt på Rhenska gulden slag» («gold coin of the Rhenish gulden kind»), 72 pieces to the 18½-carat mark, but was never executed[101]. The Nürnberg Valvationstag of 1551 found the surviving type «kun ca. 3/4 Karat» («only about ¾ carat») below the imperial norm, i.e. at 17¾ carats[72][102]; Wilcke counts this gold as soldiers' pay, reduced in fineness «til Fordel for Kongen» («to the King’s advantage»)[103]. A second type — with a mark in the small shield at the king's feet that the Valvationstag describes as a «Stern» («star»)[104] and the Museum Regium as «stella, seu potius urticæ folium» («a star, or rather a nettle leaf»)[105] — was assessed by the Valvationstag at 17 carats[106]; no specimen of it seems to survive[107].

Beskikkelsesbrev Dynes Blicher, 1 August 1513
71⅓ Goldgulden / rough Cologne mark / 18½ Karat (?)
Christian II’s letter of appointment for the mintmaster Dynes Blicher at Malmø is the first known Danish instrument on the gulden; according to Galster it is the oldest Danish Møntordning, preserved not in its wording but in a detailed summary[111]. It orders «hele og halve Gylden saa god som rinske Gylden» («whole and half gulden as good as Rhenish gulden»)[74], that is, by the imperial norm in force since the Reichsabschied of Worms 1495: 71⅓ pieces to the mark at 18½ carats[86][79]. It was not executed: according to Wilcke, neither the skilling of 1513 nor the Rhenish gulden «came to execution»[91].
Møntordning Christian II 1514
72 Goldgulden / rough Cologne mark / 18 Karat
The Møntordning of summer 1514 — part of the Christian II Lovkompleks of 1514–1515[52] — prescribes the Rhenish gulden at 72 pieces to the mark at 18 carats[37]: both lighter and half a carat lower than the Electors’ imperial norm[79]. No gulden of Christian II is known: the series of Danish Rhenish gulden passes from Hans directly to Frederik I[112].
Møntordning Frederik I 1524 (Kongebrev)
72 Goldgulden / rough Cologne mark / 18 Karat
Frederik I’s Møntordning of 1524 was issued in the form of a royal open letter, after 25 February, to the mintmaster Jørgen Kock at Malmø, with a like letter to the mintmaster at Ribe[58]; it orders the coin «over hele Riget» («throughout the whole Realm»)[60] and repeats for the Rhenish gulden the norm of 1514: 18 carats, 72 pieces[75].
Period II · 1527 → 1536
II.a · 1527 → 1531 · King Frederik I
72 Goldgulden / rough Cologne mark / 18 Karat
Under the prescription of Frederik I’s Møntordning of 1524, Jørgen Kock strikes the gulden in 1527 at Malmø[113]. The gulden of 1531 was struck at the mint at Gottorp, which according to Galster Duke Frederik had probably set up in 1522[114]; ducal coinage rested on the imperial permission of 1474 for gold and silver coin[115], whereas the Møntordning of 1524 was issued by the King with Rigets Raad for coin «over hele Riget» («throughout the whole Realm»)[60]; whether it extended to the duchies is not attested in the sources. In 1523 Frederik had promised the Rigsraad that the coin of the realm would be «eens og liig ved vores Mynt (ɔ: Hertugdømmets)» («the same and equal to our coin, i.e. the duchy’s»); according to Wilcke this refers to the rate of the silver coin, 24 ß to the gulden[116]. No norm of its own is known for the Gottorp gulden; the fineness Galster gives for it is the same — 18 carats[117][118], so it can be regarded as struck to the norm of 1524. Whether that fineness was measured or assumed from the Danish norm is not stated in the source.

II.b · 1534 → 1536 · King Christian III · Reynold Junge's account
72 Goldgulden / rough Cologne mark / 18⅓ Karat
Under Christian III the gulden are struck by Reynold Junge, who works at Gottorp from the spring of 1534; his work gets fully under way when Count Christopher opens the war with his invasion of Holstein, and there is no coinage ordinance on his appointment — only an oral agreement with the Marshal Melchior Rantzau[122]. The standard shows in his account: the gold is alloyed at 18 carats 4 grains (18⅓ carats) and cut at 72 pieces to the mark, 24,470 gulden in all[76]. Such were also the gulden of the Rhenish Electors at the time: according to Schrötter they became 18⅓-carat in the 1530s[93], and the Valvationstag of 1551 finds 72 pieces to the mark at 18⅓ carats to be the usual output of the Rhenish gulden in the Empire[123]. The Wendish towns at this time strike hardly any gulden of their own[124], but in their coinage negotiations of 1544–1546 they value precisely the Rhenish gulden «zu 72 Stück aus der Mark» («of 72 pieces to the mark»)[125]. No instrument fixing this norm is attested in the sources; Junge can be taken to have struck to the actual norm of the circulating Rhenish gulden, not to the Danish prescription. Debasing the coinage in the Duchies was not allowed, and the Netherlands money-changers valued Christian III’s gulden on a par with that of Hamburg, at 28 stuivers[126]. Striking takes place at Gottorp in 1534[127] and at Roskilde in 1536, where on 30 November 1536 some 350 ounces of gold were brought to be struck into gulden as pay for the troops[128][129]. The Recess of 1540 sets the rate: the Rhenish gulden no dearer than 3 Mark danske, on a par with the Joachimsthaler[130].

Period III · 1546 → 1547
III.a · 1546 → 1546 · King Christian III · Reynold Junge
71⅓ Goldgulden / rough Cologne mark / 18 Karat
Under Christian III the Flensborg mint is re-established in 1544 with the mintmaster Jørgen Degener; for the new mint the King asks Lübeck and Hamburg for their coinage ordinance[132]. After Degener’s death it is run from 1546 by Reynold Junge[133] — he had held the King’s permission to strike gold gulden since 1545[134]; the gulden of these years are attributed to Flensborg by his mintmark, the glowing hook[135]. No prescription for the Flensborg gulden under Junge is attested in the sources, so the standard of this coinage can only be read from the coins themselves: Wilcke’s table gives the gulden of 1546 as 71⅓ pieces to the mark at 18 carats[136] — the piece count of the Electors’ imperial norm[79], but the fineness of the Danish Møntordning of 1514[37].

III.b · 1547 → 1547 · King Christian III · letter appointing Jørgen Kock, 22 January 1547
71⅓ Goldgulden / rough Cologne mark / 18½ Karat
Reynold Junge runs the mint for about a year, until his death; his successor is the mintmaster at Skien, Jørgen Kock the younger, who on 22 January 1547 receives his appointment together with a coinage ordinance[138]. He is appointed for four years to coin for the Duchies of Schleswig and Holstein, with the mint at Flensborg[139]. The ordinance gives the Flensborg gulden a written standard for the first time: it is to be struck «efter Kurfyrsternes Gylden i Værd, Halt, Schrot og Korn» («after the Electors’ gulden in value, content, weight and fineness»)[78] — 71⅓ pieces to the mark at 18½ carats, i.e. half a carat above the fineness of the 1546 gulden[79]. A new type is known from the same year, with the arms of Holstein on the obverse[80]; its weight and fineness are not known from the sources, so whether it met the prescription remains open.

Reichsmünzordnung Ferdinand I 1559
72 Goldgulden / rough Cologne mark / 18½ Karat
The Reichsmünzordnung — the coinage edict of Emperor Ferdinand I of 19 August 1559 — finally fixes «72 Stkr. af den 18 1/2 Karat fine Mark» («72 pieces from the mark of 18½ carats fine») and at the same time, with six months' notice, declares the older gulden of Christian III and Hans invalid[81][140].
Period IV · 1563 → 1584 · King Frederik II
72 Goldgulden / rough Cologne mark / 18½ Karat
There is no new Danish gulden ordinance after 1547: the parameters of the 1559 edict, 72 pieces from the mark at 18½ carats, remained valid «helt ind i Christian IV.s Tid» («well into the time of Christian IV»)[81]. Frederik II strikes from 1563 at København to this fineness[82], and in 1584 at Frederiksborg for Queen Sophie's gold gift set[141]. The metal of 1563 is that received from Elector August of Saxony, 65,000 gulden and 26,875 thalers; in 1563–1564, during the Nordic Seven Years' War, 3,254 «Guldgylden Klippinge» («gold gulden klippes») were struck[142][143].

Period VI · 1625 → 1632 · King Christian IV · no ordinance
72 Goldgulden / rough Cologne mark / 18¼ Karat
Royal gulden coinage resumes under Christian IV at København: gulden are dated 1625, 1627, 1628 and 1632, and according to the accounts some 34,000 pieces at 1¼ Rigsdaler were struck[150][83]. No published ordinance governs this coinage: the pieces to the mark and the fineness appear from the mint accounts — 72 pieces at 18¼ carats, the very fineness the pieces show[96][84][83]. The issues of 1625–1628 are tied to the war: as early as 1625 «var givet Befaling til Udmøntning af et stort Antal Guldgylden til Lønning af Krigsfolket» («orders had been given to strike a large number of gold gulden to pay the soldiery»)[151], and this gold was, per Wilcke, somewhat reduced in fineness «til Fordel for Kongen» («to the King’s advantage»)[103]; the gulden dated 1625 were, per Harck, possibly struck as early as 1624 and dated forward[152], and the pieces of 1627 and 1628 are, per Harck, «oplagt slået til anvendelse i forbindelse med trediveårskrigen» («evidently struck for use in connection with the Thirty Years’ War»)[98]. The coinage of 1632, per Harck, has quite a different background: Christian IV had already concluded a separate peace with the Emperor in 1629[153].
