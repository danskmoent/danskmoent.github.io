Phase 0 · 1496 → 1510 · Before the first Danish ordinance · benchmark: the electors' norm of 1490 and the Reichsabschied of Worms 1495 — 71⅓ pieces at 18½ carats · assayed fineness 17¾ carats
The coinage of Hans (r. 1481-1513) precedes any Danish gold ordinance. Its benchmark is the norm of the four Rhenish electors of 1490 — 71⅓ pieces from the rough Cologne mark at 18½ carats — made binding throughout the Empire by the Reichsabschied of Worms in 1495[7][8]. The undated gulden from Malmø or København, «formentlig slået omkring 1497», paid the German mercenaries in the war against Sten Sture[17]; according to Hvitfeldt the king had 150,000 struck for the campaign of 1497[25]. Hans also struck the half gulden[23] and at Bergen a gulden reading REX NORWEGIE / MONETA NOVA AVREA BERGE[26]. His Møntanordning for Sweden of 4 December 1497 prescribes «guldmynt på Rhenska gulden slag», 72 pieces to the 18½-carat mark, but was never executed[27]. The Nürnberg Valvationstag of 1551 found the surviving type «kun ca. 3/4 Karat» below the norm, i.e. at 17¾ carats[19]; Wilcke counts this gold as soldiers' pay, reduced in fineness «til Fordel for Kongen»[28].

Phase I · 1514 → 1547 · Letter of appointment 1513 · Møntordning of summer 1514 · Kongebrev 1524 — 72 pieces from the rough Cologne mark at 18 carats
The first Danish instrument on the gulden — Christian II's letter of appointment for the mintmaster Dynes Blicher of 1 August 1513 — orders «hele og halve Gylden saa god som rinske Gylden», but was not executed[12]. What took effect was the Møntordning of summer 1514: 72 pieces from the mark at 18 carats, half a carat below the imperial norm[13]; Frederik I repeated it in the royal letter (Kongebrev) of 1524[14]. Under this prescription Frederik I strikes at Malmø in 1527[29] and Christian III at Roskilde in 1536[30]; the gulden of 1536 hold .764, i.e. 18⅓ carats — the figure the Valvationstag of 1551 records as the usual actual fineness of the Rhenish gulden in the Empire[31]. At Flensborg Christian III strikes the single and double gulden in 1546[24]; the bestalling there of 22 January 1547 orders the gulden «efter Kurfyrsternes Gylden», i.e. to the imperial norm[32], and the Flensborg pieces weigh to the imperial grid of 71⅓ to the mark (as computed from 3.278 g). The Recess of 1540 sets the rate: the Rhenish gulden no higher than 3 Mark danske, equal to the Joachimsthaler[33].

Phase II · 1563 → 1584 · Coinage edict of Emperor Ferdinand I of 19 August 1559 — 72 pieces to the mark at 18½ carats · 2.50367 g fine per piece
The coinage edict of Emperor Ferdinand I of 19 August 1559 finally fixes «72 Stkr. af den 18 1/2 Karat fine Mark» and at the same time, with six months' notice, declares the older gulden of Christian III and Hans invalid[11]. There is no new Danish gulden ordinance after 1547; Frederik II strikes from 1563 at København to the edict's fineness, 18½ carats[11][20], and in 1584 at Frederiksborg for Queen Sophie's gold gift set[34]. The metal of 1563 came from a loan of Elector August of Saxony, 65,000 gulden and 26,875 thalers; in the same year 3,254 «Guldgylden Klippinge» were struck for the troops of the Nordic Seven Years' War[35].

Phase III · 1619 → 1627 · Ducal lines · Gottorp and the Sonderburg lines as vassals of the Danish crown · no ordinance of their own, fineness not given
Gottorp and the Sonderburg cadet lines struck the Goldgulden to the Rhenish norm from 1619[16]. The dukes had no ordinance of their own, and for none of these coinages do the sources give the fineness; the benchmark is the imperial norm then in force, the edict of 1559 — 72 pieces from the mark at 18½ carats[11].

Phase IV · 1625 → 1632 · Christian IV · no ordinance of its own · the coinage edict of 19 August 1559 (18½ carats) remains in force · fineness as found 18¼ carats
Under Christian IV coinage resumes at København: gulden are dated 1625, 1627, 1628 and 1632; according to the accounts some 34,000 pieces at 1¼ Rigsdaler were struck[15][21]. As early as 1625 «var givet Befaling til Udmøntning af et stort Antal Guldgylden til Lønning af Krigsfolket»[22]; the pieces of 1627 and 1628 are, per Harck, «oplagt slået til anvendelse i forbindelse med trediveårskrigen»[15]. No ordinance for this coinage survives; the relations of the 1559 edict remained valid «helt ind i Christian IV.s Tid»[11]. The fineness of 18¼ carats is not a prescription but what the pieces show[21][22]; Wilcke counts this gold as soldiers' pay, reduced in fineness «til Fordel for Kongen»[28]. The Forordning of 8 September 1602 no longer provides for the Rhenish gulden[18]; the coinage stands outside any Danish prescription.
