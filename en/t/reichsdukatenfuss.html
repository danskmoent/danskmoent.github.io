Phase I · 1531 → 1601 · Ungersk Gylden and Portugaløser — the gold-gulden naming of the .986 gold (Frederik I to Christian IV)
Early .986 gold coinage of Denmark-Norway under the names Ungersk Gylden and Portugaløser. The earliest documented piece is Frederik I's Ungersk Gylden of 1531 (Galster 46); the Hans gold gulden ~1497 belongs to the Rhinskgyldenfod at .75, not this standard. Christian III follows in 1557 (Hede c3h1/c3h2) and Frederik II in 1563-1564; Christian IV takes the line up with the Haderslev Portugaløsere and Goldgulden (1591-1593). Across this stretch the fineness is .986 — metrically identical to the German Reichsdukatenfuß codified by the Augsburg Imperial Coinage Ordinance of 1559, which however did not bind the Kingdom of Denmark. The ordinance of 8 September 1602 ends that alignment by setting the Ungersk Gylden at 23⅓ carats[32] — see the following section.

Phase II · 1602 → 1611 · The gold standard of the ordinance of 8 September 1602 — Ungersk Gylden 23⅓, Portugaløser 23½ carats
The ordinance of 8 September 1602 does not fix the gold at one fineness but sets it separately per denomination: the Portugaløser at 23½ carats (.979), the Ungersk Gylden at 23⅓ carats (.972) — four grains below the imperial ducat fineness of 23 carats 8 grains. The rough weight stays unchanged at 67 pieces per mark, so the fine gold per piece falls from 3.442 g to 3.393 g. Wilcke reads the step as a deliberate reduction: «Med disse Forhold for Øje er det forklarligt, om man noget forringede Mønten»[32]. Both pieces also carry a statutory face value in silver daler — 17 for the Portugaløser, 1⅝ for the Ungersk Gylden. On 20 November 1604 those rates were reset (to 16 and 1 9/16), the fineness left alone[39]. The ordinance of 3 February 1609 leaves the gold untouched; the section runs out with the last dated Ungersk Gylden.

Phase III · 1623 → 1726 · Late Portugaløsere and ducats at 23½ carats — Copenhagen, Christiania, Glückstadt (Christian IV to Frederik IV)
The later gold of the standard. The phase opens with Christian IV's final Portugaløser prestige pieces (1 Portugaløser 1623, ¼ Portugaløser 1629) and the emerging ducat coinage — the 1627 Wolfenbüttel ducat of the Danish occupation coinage of the Thirty Years' War. The naming shifts: the coin is called Ungersk Gylden down to the last dated pieces of 1611, and from the 1640s Hede lists it as Dukat. Across this whole stretch the fineness stands at 23½ carats (.979) — the fineness the ordinance of 1602 gave the Portugaløser[34] — in Copenhagen as much as in Christiania, Glückstadt and Rendsborg[33][36]. For the Glückstadt pieces Krause carries the imperial 23 carats 8 grains instead[37]; the divergence is recorded under the base values. In 1726 the line passes into the Courant union (Phase IV).
